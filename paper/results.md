# Results

I first compare mechanisms in one population, then in two. I next raise information
cost when both populations pay alike, then let them differ in who pays that cost,
and finally vary both information costs together.

## 1. One population

I start with a single population across the Cost × c triangle (Fig. S1). In the
prisoner's dilemma, cooperation stays near zero irrespective of cost in the
no-enforcement control (Fig. S1a,f). Cooperation collapses at successively higher
cooperation costs under direct reciprocity M (Fig. S1c), short-memory partner choice P
(Fig. S1b), and the combined mechanism IJMPQ (Fig. S1e), in that order, and that
ordering shrinks as information cost rises. The low-information-cost edge of that
triangle matches the one-dimensional cost ceilings in Fig. S7.

Raising c decreases R and S and leaves T and P unchanged (Table 1), so a single
cooperation-cost sweep cannot say which payoff drives the fall in cooperation. I
run a separate set of simulations that vary those payoffs independently
(Table S1). A mechanism is risk-limited if its cooperation tracks P − S across
those sweeps, and reward-limited if it tracks the mutual-cooperation payoff R
rather than the other payoffs. In the prisoner's dilemma, P > S, so mimicking a
defector is the safe reply (it earns P instead of S). Direct reciprocity (M) is
risk-limited: the fall in cooperation as c rises tracks the growing
mutual-defection risk rather than the shrinking R − P. In snowdrift, S > P, so
there is no risk and mimicking a defector is costly (it earns the worst payoff P
instead of S). The same columns under snowdrift (Fig. S2; low-i ceilings Fig. S9)
show more cooperation without enforcement — unconditional cooperators keep
cooperating while reciprocity alleles are selected against — and the ordering among
mechanisms flattens. Partner choice (P) is limited by the cooperation advantage
R − P in both games: it tracks R − P alone across those independent sweeps, which
is why it fails as R − P → 0. The combined mechanisms MP, MPQ, IMP, and IJMPQ are
reward-limited and do not track the mutual-defection payoff P, which is why they
sustain cooperation to the highest costs (Fig. S1e; Fig. S7e). Under shuffled
partnerships the same cost sweep for M, MP, IM, and IMP collapses direct
reciprocity, while short-memory indirect reciprocity and the partner-choice
combinations still sustain cooperation farther (in both the prisoner's dilemma and
snowdrift); partner choice alone is nearly unchanged by shuffle. Those shuffle-only
branches are not shown as separate figures.

## 2. Symmetric and asymmetric cooperation cost in two populations

Two coevolving populations often settle into a cooperator role and an exploiter
role, and the population that cooperates more earns less fitness. Whether those
roles appear depends on the game and on the enforcement mechanism.

Fig. S7 places one population (top row), symmetric costs in two populations
(middle row, greens), and a small difference in cooperation costs
c₁ = c₀ + 0.02 (bottom row, orange/red) on shared axes under the prisoner's
dilemma; columns are no enforcement, partner choice (P), direct reciprocity (M),
and combined IJMPQ. Without enforcement, symmetric-cost populations both stay near
defection and take no roles (Fig. S7f), whereas that cost difference alone still assigns no
roles (Fig. S7k; cheap-side mean cooperation 0.10 across the full c₀ × c₁ grid,
expensive-side 0.03). Partner choice splits the symmetric-cost populations
stochastically: one cooperates more and is exploited, and the differences in cooperation and
fitness correlate at −0.98, so the population that cooperates more earns
less — the paradox of success (Fig. S7g; fitness Fig. S8). The same cost difference makes that
assignment deterministic
along the strip (Fig. S7l). Direct reciprocity and IJMPQ leave both populations
cooperating at similar frequencies under symmetric costs and under asymmetric costs, with no
cooperator/exploiter split: each side cooperates only as much as its partner does
(Fig. S7h,i,m,n). Under M and IJMPQ on the asymmetric-cost strip their fitness differs only by
the 0.02 cost difference (Fig. S8m,n). Under partner choice the cheap population cooperates
more in all 210 cells of the full grid and earns less fitness in all of them
(differences in cooperation and fitness correlate at −0.99; Fig. 1; Fig. S12j). Direct reciprocity
keeps the cheap side cooperating more in every cell but reverses the fitness ranking
over most of the triangle: the cheap population earns more fitness in 174 of 210
cells (Fig. S12k). Under IJMPQ the fitness difference mixes both signs (Fig. S12l). The split
also stays deterministic in groups of 4, where each population samples few partners
per round.

## 3. Symmetric information cost

Individuals pay an information cost for each mechanism family they carry, whether or
not they express it (Methods). From here on, most figures show partner choice (P) and
IJMPQ as the two mechanism families — assortment alone versus assortment with
reciprocity — while Figs. 2–3 (and their snowdrift twins) also keep direct reciprocity
(M) on the information-cost planes. Other combinations are summarized in the text
where they matter for the unit-cost comparison (Results §7).

The same numerical value is harsher as a cooperation cost than as an information
cost: at c = 0.40 with a nearly free mechanism, partner choice and direct reciprocity
both collapse (Fig. S7b,c), yet IJMPQ at an information cost of 0.20 — paying 0.40
per round because it carries both families — still cooperates at high frequency
(Fig. S3; one population Fig. S1). Where cooperation is free (c = 0, so T = R),
information cost selects against the chooser and reciprocity alleles while
populations keep cooperating unconditionally, so cooperation frequency and
mechanism-allele frequency diverge; once every individual pays a cooperation cost,
alleles and behavior fall together.

The information cost lowers the cooperation cost a population can withstand. Under
IJMPQ in the prisoner's dilemma, both populations hold to high c when the mechanism
is nearly free and collapse at far lower c once the information cost is 0.20
(Fig. S3; fitness Fig. S4). Snowdrift softens that interaction: the higher-cooperation
population stays high across the grid, including the cell where the prisoner's dilemma
collapses, while the lower-cooperation population can still fall in the interior
(Fig. S5; fitness Fig. S6). Because S > P, cooperation is favored without enforcement,
so losing the alleles costs less than in the prisoner's dilemma.

The difference rows show how symmetric costs still assign roles. In the prisoner's dilemma,
partner choice opens a stochastic difference in cooperation across much of the Cost × c plane,
while direct reciprocity and IJMPQ keep both sides close (Fig. S3j–l). In snowdrift,
the no-enforcement default already opens a large difference once c > 0, and partner choice
does not change that pattern; M narrows that difference and IJMPQ shrinks it further,
especially when information cost is low (Fig. S5i–l).

A symmetric information cost cannot separate the cost a population pays from the burden
that cost places on its partner. Both populations pay the same information cost in
every cell above, so a fall in cooperation cannot be assigned to the payer or to its
partner. The next sections vary information cost across populations — first alone,
then together with asymmetric cooperation costs.

## 4. Populations that differ only in information cost

When cooperation costs are symmetric (c₀ = c₁ = 0.1) and only information costs differ
(i₀ < i₁), which population cooperates depends on the mechanism (Fig. 2). Under
partner choice the lower-i population cooperates more in 63/120 cells (mean difference
+0.110; Fig. 2b,f,j): it keeps the chooser allele while the higher-i side sheds it
and defects, yet still wins fitness (fitness Fig. S16). At (i₀, i₁) = (0, 0.20) the
frequencies of cooperators are 0.331 / 0.153. Without enforcement the triangle is
flat near zero (Fig. 2a,e,i).

Under IJMPQ the assignment reverses: the higher-i population cooperates more in
85/120 cells (mean difference −0.201; Fig. 2d,h,l). Reciprocity-bearing mechanisms protect
only their carriers, so the expensive side can switch to unconditional cooperation
that pays no information cost and hitchhike on the cheap side's remaining alleles —
the expensive population cooperates more while the cheap one earns more. Direct
reciprocity alone places the same reverse assignment (Fig. 2c,g,k). Snowdrift removes
these PD locks (Fig. S17; fitness Fig. S18).

## 5. Symmetric information cost under asymmetric cooperation costs

Fig. S19 holds the asymmetric cooperation costs of section 2 and raises a symmetric information
cost: the partner-choice split shrinks as that cost rises, rather than reversing
(fitness Fig. S20). IJMPQ postpones that collapse but does not restore the
free-cooperation outcome. In snowdrift (Fig. S21; fitness Fig. S22) the cheap
population stays near full cooperation under partner choice and IJMPQ even at high
information cost, while the expensive population remains the exploiter; IJMPQ raises
that side at low information cost and then loses it as the cost rises. Because both
populations still pay the same information cost in every cell, a fall in cooperation
still cannot be assigned to the payer or to its partner. Holding one population's
information cost at zero and sweeping the other's separates the two.

## 6. Independent per-population information costs under asymmetric cooperation costs

Under partner choice, with fixed asymmetric cooperation costs (c₀ = 0.1, c₁ = 0.2) and
independent per-population information costs, a population barely bears a burden from
its own information cost and cooperates much less under the burden of its partner's
(Fig. 3b,f,j). Along the bottom edge of that plane (i₁ = 0,
i₀ swept) the cheap population's frequency of cooperators barely changes as its own
information cost rises from 0 to 0.20 (0.602 → 0.585) and its partner's even rises
(0.189 → 0.268). Along the left edge (i₀ = 0, i₁ swept) the same rise paid by the
high-cooperation-cost population collapses both sides (0.069 in the payer; 0.032 in
its partner). The bilateral swap rule is why: a chooser is useless without a chooser
to trade with, so assortment is limited by whichever population has fewest choosers —
and that is already the high-cost side before any information cost is applied.

Under IJMPQ the pattern reverses (Fig. 3d,h,l). When the
low-cooperation-cost population's information cost rises from i = 0 to i = 0.20, its
partner's frequency of cooperators falls from 0.957 to 0.268 while the payer itself
falls only to 0.734 — the partner bears a larger burden than the payer. When the
high-cost side pays that rise instead, that side's frequency of cooperators stays near
0.911 (and its partner remains high). Reciprocity-bearing mechanisms — any mechanism
carrying an I, J, or M locus — protect only their carriers: a population that pays
information cost can switch to unconditional cooperation, which is harmless when that
side contributes little cooperation and destructive when it carries most of it. Here
the partner bears a larger burden when the *low*-cooperation-cost population pays the
information cost — the opposite of partner choice.

Genotype composition confirms the same point: a partner's cost can leave an allele
common yet unused in a population that pays no information cost. At
(i₀, i₁) = (0, 0.20) that population's P1 frequency sits at its mutation-drift value
(P1 = 0.519; inert Q1 = 0.498, M1 = 0.504) because the allele is not expressed and
therefore not under selection, yet little of it is expressed (C1P1 = 0.044, against
0.187 in the same population when it pays the 0.20 information cost itself). With the
partner's choosers selected out there is nobody to swap with; the population falls to
defection and the allele survives only in defector carriers that never choose. Partner
choice therefore assorts at the population level — a residual chooser minority can
still sort both sides — while reciprocity protects only its carriers.

The pattern that lets a population keep cooperating after losing mechanism alleles
under symmetry is what places a burden on its partner under asymmetry: losing
mechanism alleles relieves the payer and removes the assortment or conditional help
the partner needed.

## 7. Both information costs vary

Across the full i₀ × i₁ square (Fig. 3; fitness Fig. S24; snowdrift Fig. S25), under
partner choice the
lower-cooperation-cost population cooperates more than its partner in 170/176 cells;
under IJMPQ the expensive population's cooperation exceeds the cheap one's only
locally, on the strip where i₀ ≤ 0.02 (13 cells; Fig. 3l). That pattern is stronger
when populations differ only in information cost (Fig. 2) and weaker once
cooperation costs are asymmetric (cooperation difference −0.100 at i₀ = 0, i₁ = 0.20). The
cooperation-cost asymmetry moves every reciprocity-bearing mechanism toward the
cheap-cooperation-cost population, so MP and MPQ never place the expensive side ahead
once costs differ. Direct reciprocity alone (Fig. 3c,g,k) places the same
near-zero-i₀ flip in a thinner strip (10 cells), so the IJMPQ inversion tracks the M
locus rather than partner choice. Without enforcement the plane is flat near zero
(Fig. 3a,e,i).

Under partner choice the cheap population stays ahead across the plane (Fig. 3j).
Under IJMPQ the expensive side's lead occupies only the near-zero-i₀ strip and is
gone once i₀ ≥ 0.04 (Fig. 3l). The snowdrift twin (Fig. S25) does not show this
near-zero-i₀ regime. The transition into the state without mechanism alleles is
abrupt and bistable rather than gradual: just inside this regime, raising
population 1's information cost from 0.02 to 0.04 *raises* its frequency of
cooperators by 0.32 as it switches from a defector-heavy mixed state into
near-complete unconditional cooperation that pays no information cost. Run-to-run
SD locates that boundary — wide below the threshold (SD ≈ 0.25; runs diverge to
different outcomes) and narrow above it (SD ≈ 0.009). On the fitness plane
(Fig. S24; snowdrift Fig. S26), the same cooperation-fitness mismatch holds: the
population with higher cooperation is the one with lower fitness, even when the
color ordering switches as i₀ rises. In this threshold regime, increasing
population 1's own information cost can confer a fitness benefit on population 0
even while population 1 becomes slightly less fit (Fig. S24), showing that one
population can benefit from a partner that pays more for enforcement alleles.

The same total information cost does not yield the same cooperation: equal totals are
neither interchangeable nor additive (Fig. S27). Fig. S27 takes the fixed-total cut
through Fig. 3 (i₀ + i₁ = 0.20, x = i₁); columns are — / P / M / IJMPQ. Along that cut
every reciprocity-bearing mechanism reaches its *lowest* total cooperation — the sum
of the two populations' frequencies of cooperators, on a 0–2 scale — when the total is
split between populations rather than paid by one side alone. Under IJMPQ populations
that place a total of 0.20 on one side reach 1.0–1.65 total cooperation, but only 0.39
when they split it as (0.06, 0.14) (Fig. S27d). Either end leaves one population still
carrying mechanism alleles, which is enough to sustain cooperation in both; a split
reduces both populations' mechanism alleles so that neither remains above the
threshold at which cooperation without those alleles takes over. Partner choice does
not show that interior minimum (Fig. S27b): because only the high-cooperation-cost
population's information cost matters, total cooperation is highest when that
population pays none of the total. That non-additivity is therefore a property of
reciprocity-bearing mechanisms, not of the total itself. The fitness row of the same
cut shows the same cross-population burdens and the same interior minimum under a
fixed total (Fig. S27e–h; snowdrift Fig. S28; plane Fig. S24).

Whether the expensive population ends up cooperating more than the cheap one depends
on whether the mechanism includes reciprocity, not on how much information cost it
pays. Information cost is charged per family carried — one unit for partner choice,
one for reciprocity — so partner choice and direct reciprocity each pay one unit, and
MP, MPQ, IMP and IJMPQ each pay two (MP, MPQ and IMP are not shown). Yet with
i₀ ≤ 0.02, the expensive population overtakes the cheap one somewhere under direct
reciprocity and under every combination containing it, and nowhere under partner
choice alone. Once active reciprocity alleles become unaffordable, populations fall
back on cost-free unconditional cooperators (C1M0) — the second-order free-rider
genotype — whereas partner choice's bilateral swap collapses when either side loses
choosers, so P has no comparable cost-free high-cooperation outcome. Partner choice's
cooperation curve does cross above its partner's in three cells, but in the opposite
corner of the square and while its own alleles are falling, not through that
reciprocity free-rider route.

Snowdrift has no risk (S > P) and removes this near-zero-i₀ regime entirely on the
same crossed design (Fig. S25), identifying this cross-population burden as a
property of game type rather than of the cost accounting. Shuffled partnerships
leave the main contrasts intact (Results §1). The cooperator/exploiter split also
stays deterministic in groups of 4.
