# Abstract

Models explain cooperation with reciprocity, partner choice, and reputation, but treat enforcement machinery as free. In mutualisms, an information cost paid by one population can burden or benefit both. I use an individual-based evolutionary model that separates cooperation cost (the cost of helping) from information cost and varies game, ecology, and mechanism. Under partner choice in the prisoner's dilemma, one population exploits the other even when their costs are identical. When populations differ in cooperation cost, the one that helps more cheaply becomes the exploited and earns less fitness. When they differ in information cost, these roles can reverse. Crucially, a population often bears a larger burden from its partner's information cost than from its own. Under partner choice, a population cooperates if its partner can afford choosers; under reciprocity, an information cost paid by the cheap-helper population burdens its high-cost partner more than the payer. Equal budgets are not interchangeable: both populations sharing a fixed total information cost can cooperate less than when one pays it all. Snowdrift and no-dilemma games weaken this cross-population burden, yielding more symmetrical outcomes. Ask who bears the burden of an information cost, not only who pays it.
