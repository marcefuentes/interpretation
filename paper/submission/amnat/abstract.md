# Abstract

Cooperation is often explained with reciprocity, partner choice, and reputation, but models usually treat the informational machinery behind enforcement as free. In mutualisms the same information cost can affect both populations, not only the payer. We use an individual-based evolutionary model that separates cooperation cost from per-population information cost and varies game structure, ecology, and mechanism type. How a population behaves often depends more on its partner's information cost than on its own. Cooperation-cost asymmetry remains the primary role assigner; information-cost asymmetry mostly modulates that baseline and inverts roles only when the low-cooperation-cost population's information cost is near zero. Under partner choice, cooperation depends mainly on whether the partner can afford active choosers. Under reciprocity-bearing mechanisms, taxing the low-cooperation-cost population can damage its high-cost partner more than the payer. Equal total information-cost budgets are not interchangeable: splitting a fixed total between populations can yield less cooperation than concentrating it on one side. Cooperation and fitness repeatedly decouple. One-population patterns, including machinery shedding at zero cooperation cost, miss the cross-population burden dominant under the prisoner's dilemma; snowdrift and no-dilemma controls weaken these effects. The results shift attention from enforcement cost size to how its burden is distributed across interacting populations.
