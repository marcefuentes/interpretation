## American Naturalist style

**Title**  
Who pays the information cost of enforcement determines cooperation outcomes in mutualisms

**Abstract**  
Cooperation is often explained with enforcement mechanisms such as reciprocity, partner choice and reputation, but models usually treat their informational machinery as free once it exists. In mutualisms this is a strong simplification, because the same information cost can have consequences for both populations, not only for the one that pays it. We test this with an individual-based evolutionary model that separates cooperation cost from per-population information cost and varies game structure, ecology and mechanism type. The main finding is simple: how a population behaves often depends more on its partner's information cost than on its own. Cooperation-cost asymmetry remains the primary role assigner across mechanisms; information-cost asymmetry mostly modulates that baseline, and role inversion appears only when the low-cooperation-cost population's information cost is near zero. Under partner choice, cooperation is set mainly by whether the partner population can afford enough active choosers. Under reciprocity-bearing mechanisms, the cross-population burden becomes asymmetric: it is specifically the information cost paid by the low-cooperation-cost population that can damage its high-cost partner more than the payer, whereas the reverse direction is weaker. This difference also explains why two allocations of the same total information cost can produce very different outcomes, and why splitting a fixed total between populations can be worse than concentrating it on one side. Across these regimes, cooperation and fitness repeatedly come apart: the more cooperative population is often the less fit one. Familiar one-population results still appear, including machinery shedding when cooperation cost is zero, but they miss the cross-population burden that dominates in two-population interactions under the prisoner's dilemma. Snowdrift and no-dilemma controls weaken these effects, showing that they depend on genuine dilemma structure. The results therefore shift attention from the size of enforcement costs alone to how the burden created by those costs is distributed across interacting populations.

## Journal of Evolutionary Biology style

**Title**  
Information costs of enforcement have cross-population consequences in two-population cooperation

**Abstract**  
Reciprocity, partner choice and reputation can stabilize cooperation, but models often treat the informational machinery behind these mechanisms as free and focus on a single population. We analyze a two-population evolutionary model in which cooperation cost and information cost are separated, and information cost is assigned per population. We vary game type (prisoner's dilemma, snowdrift), cooperation-cost asymmetry, information-cost asymmetry, group size, shuffling and mechanism class. The main result is that the burden created by information cost often extends beyond the population that pays that cost. Cooperation-cost asymmetry remains the primary role assigner across mechanisms; information-cost asymmetry mostly modulates that baseline, and role inversion appears only when the low-cooperation-cost population's information cost is near zero. Under partner choice, cooperation in one population depends strongly on whether the partner population can afford active choosers. Under reciprocity-bearing mechanisms, increasing the information cost paid by the low-cooperation-cost population can impose a larger burden on its partner than on itself. These mechanism-specific patterns help explain why two distributions of the same total information cost can produce very different outcomes, including cases where sharing a fixed total between populations is worse than putting it all on one side. We also repeatedly find that cooperation and fitness do not move together: the more cooperative population is often the less fit one. One-population decoupling at zero cooperation cost is recovered, but it does not capture the main two-population effect, because it cannot distinguish the cost a population pays from the burden that cost creates for its partner. Together, these results identify the distribution of enforcement burden as an important evolutionary variable in mutualistic cooperation.

## Journal of Theoretical Biology style

**Title**  
Two-population evolutionary cooperation with information costs: who pays and who bears the burden

**Abstract**  
We study an individual-based two-population model of cooperation with explicit enforcement machinery (partner-choice and reciprocity/reputation loci) and per-family information costs. The design separates cooperation cost from information cost, allowing the two populations to differ independently in both. Across variation in dilemma structure, ecology and parameter asymmetry, we find that the effects of information cost cannot be understood only by asking which population pays that cost. The more informative question is which population bears the burden created by that cost. Cooperation-cost asymmetry remains the primary role assigner across mechanisms; information-cost asymmetry mostly modulates that baseline, and role inversion appears only when the low-cooperation-cost population's information cost is near zero. Under partner choice, cooperation in one population depends mainly on whether its partner can still afford active choosers. Under reciprocity-bearing mechanisms, the burden can shift so that raising the information cost paid by the low-cooperation-cost population harms its partner more than the payer. This mechanism-specific difference explains why equal totals of information cost are not equivalent to equal outcomes, and why splitting a fixed total between populations can reduce cooperation more than concentrating it on one side. We also find repeated separation between cooperation and fitness: in many parameter combinations, the more cooperative population is the less fit one. The known one-population result at zero cooperation cost is recovered, but it is not sufficient for two-population inference because it cannot distinguish payment of a cost from the burden that follows from it. Snowdrift and no-dilemma controls weaken or remove these effects, showing that they depend on dilemma-coupled cooperation.
